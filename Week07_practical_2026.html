The Bjerrum plot and the pH of rain

Uses the lecture: The carbonate system: three equilibria, Dissolved inorganic carbon and pH, and The pH of rain

In the lecture you saw the three equilibria between dissolved CO₂, bicarbonate and carbonate. Which form is most abundant depends only on the pH: that is what a Bjerrum plot shows. Here is one you can play with.

[HCO₃⁻][H₂CO₃*] = K₁[H⁺]      [CO₃²⁻][H₂CO₃*] = K₁ × K₂[H⁺]²

Both come from rearranging the expressions for K₁ and K₂. [H⁺]: H⁺ concentration (mol/L), = 10−pH. K₁, K₂: dissociation constants (mol/L). The ratios have no units. The plot below is these two ratios, turned into fractions that add up to 1.

The pH of rain

As in the lecture: rain is pure water in equilibrium with the air, with no other acids or bases. Use your calculator, or a spreadsheet.

[H₂CO₃*] = KH × pCO₂      [H⁺] = √(K₁ × [H₂CO₃*])      pH = −log10[H⁺]

pCO₂: partial pressure of CO₂ (atm). At 1 atm, 1 ppm is 10−6 atm. KH = 10−1.47 mol L−1 atm−1 and K₁ = 10−6.35 mol/L at 25 °C. [H₂CO₃*]: dissolved CO₂ and carbonic acid (mol/L). The square root comes from the charge balance: [H⁺] = [HCO₃⁻], so [H⁺]² = K₁ × [H₂CO₃*].

Worked example: The pH of water under a different gas

Water in equilibrium with 1000 ppm of CO₂ at 25 °C.

  1. pCO₂ = 1000 × 10−6 atm = 1.0 × 10−3 atm.
  2. [H₂CO₃*] = 0.0339 mol L−1 atm−1 × 1.0 × 10−3 atm = 3.39 × 10−5 mol/L.
  3. [H⁺] = √(4.47 × 10−7 mol/L × 3.39 × 10−5 mol/L) = 3.89 × 10−6 mol/L.
  4. pH = −log10(3.89 × 10−6) = 5.41.

Alkalinity in µeq/L

To compare alkalinity with the ions, it has to be in units of charge. CaCO₃ has a molar mass of 100.09 g/mol. Each mole of CaCO₃ contains one mole of CO₃²⁻, which has a charge of 2−, so it can neutralise two moles of H⁺ (CO₃²⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂CO₃*): 1 mmol of CaCO₃ is 2 meq of alkalinity.

Alkalinity (µeq/L) = alkalinity (mg/L as CaCO₃)100.09 mg/mmol × 2 × 1000
Step 3
Convert the alkalinity of two streams to µeq/L:
Σ cations = Na⁺ + K⁺ + 2 × Mg²⁺ + 2 × Ca²⁺      Σ anions of strong acids = Cl⁻ + 2 × SO₄²⁻ + NO₃⁻

All in µeq/L: µmol/L times the charge. A water has no net charge, so the cations not balanced by the anions of strong acids must be balanced by something else: in most waters, by HCO₃⁻, which is the alkalinity.

CO₂ in the streams

Is the stream water in equilibrium with the air? From the pH and the alkalinity, you can work out how much CO₂ is dissolved in it, and the pCO₂ of air it would be in equilibrium with. Streams are cool, so use the constants at 10 °C, in cells B13 and B14. Below pH 8.3, almost all of the alkalinity is HCO₃⁻.

[H₂CO₃*] = [H⁺] × [HCO₃⁻]K₁      pCO₂ = [H₂CO₃*]KH

[H⁺] = 10−pH (mol/L). [HCO₃⁻]: the alkalinity in mol/L (µeq/L × 10−6, as HCO₃⁻ has one charge). K₁ (mol/L) and KH (mol L−1 atm−1) at 10 °C. pCO₂ in atm; × 106 for ppm. The air in 1992–2015 averaged 377.3 ppm (cell B15).

Extra: why the pCO₂ of Moor House A is meaningless, and that of Snowdon and the Cairngorms uncertain

Moor House A. In Step 4, [HCO₃⁻] is taken from the alkalinity, and [H₂CO₃*] is then worked out from [HCO₃⁻] and the pH. At pH 4.3, more than 99 % of the DIC is H₂CO₃* (pK₁ is 6.46 at 10 °C), so there is almost no HCO₃⁻, and neither way of measuring it works:

  • The alkalinity titration adds acid until the pH reaches about 4.5. This water starts below that, so its alkalinity is recorded as zero, although there is dissolved CO₂ in it.
  • The charge balance doesn't help either: the missing negative charge (question e) is made up of organic anions from the peat, not HCO₃⁻.

So for this stream, the dissolved CO₂ has to be measured directly. Either measure the DIC: acidify a sample in a sealed vial, so that all of it is converted to CO₂, and measure that CO₂ with an infrared gas analyser. At pH 4.3 almost all of the DIC is H₂CO₃*, so pCO₂ = DIC ÷ KH. Or measure the pCO₂ itself: shake stream water with a known volume of air in a syringe until the two are in equilibrium, and measure the CO₂ in the air (the headspace method), or put a CO₂ sensor in the stream.

Snowdon and the Cairngorms. Their alkalinity is small, about 6 and 46 µeq/L. A titration error of a few µeq/L, or organic anions titrated as if they were HCO₃⁻, is a large share of that, and pCO₂ is proportional to it. The pH of such poorly buffered water is also hard to measure: an error of 0.1 pH units is a 26 % error in [H⁺], and so in pCO₂. So their pCO₂ values are rough: read them as 'probably above that of the air', not as exact numbers.

Spreadsheet skill: the slope of a trend

=SLOPE(y values, x values) gives the slope of the straight line that best fits the points: here, the change per year. =FORECAST(2100, y values, x values) gives where that line reaches in 2100. Both work the same in Excel and Google Sheets. The trend line on your chart shows the same slope.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Partial pressureThe share of a gas in a mixture, times the total pressure. For CO₂ in air at 1 atm, ppm × 10−6 atm.
Henry's lawAt equilibrium, the concentration of a dissolved gas is proportional to its partial pressure.
H₂CO₃*Dissolved CO₂ and true carbonic acid, counted together, because they can't be told apart.
DICDissolved inorganic carbon: all three carbonate species.
Bjerrum plotThe share of each species in the DIC, against pH.
AlkalinityThe capacity of a water to neutralise acid; mostly HCO₃⁻ and CO₃²⁻.
Equivalent (eq)An amount of charge: one mole of a singly charged ion. 1 µmol of Ca²⁺ is 2 µeq.
Saturation state, Ω[Ca²⁺][CO₃²⁻] ÷ Ksp: above 1, supersaturated; below 1, undersaturated.
Lysocline, CCDDepths in the ocean below which CaCO₃ starts to dissolve noticeably, and below which none is buried.
Ocean acidificationThe fall in seawater pH and Ω as CO₂ from the air is taken up by the ocean.
