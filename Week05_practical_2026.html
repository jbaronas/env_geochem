Make a guess if you don't know. Not all of these elements were covered in the lecture, and you aren't expected to know them all. There are no marks for this, and the answer appears as soon as all ten are placed.

How can a soil gain an element? Weathering on its own only removes material, so a gain means that the element was added from somewhere else. It can be added by windblown dust or sea spray. It can be added by plants: roots take up nutrients at depth, and their litter returns those nutrients to the surface. It can also be precipitated from soil water that carried it down from higher in the profile.

Spreadsheet skill: one step per column

Leave the raw data as it is and build to the right, with a clear header on each column. To turn an oxide into its element, you need these columns:

ColumnWhat it is (units)
nnumber of atoms of the element in the oxide's formula: Al₂O₃ has n = 2 (no units)
mnumber of oxygen atoms in the oxide's formula: Al₂O₃ has m = 3 (no units)
Melementatomic mass of the element (g/mol)
MOatomic mass of oxygen, 15.999 (g/mol)
Moxidemolar mass of the oxide = n × Melement + m × MO (g/mol)
factorshare of the oxide's mass that is the element = n × Melement ÷ Moxide (no units)
element wt%grams of the element per 100 g of sample = oxide wt% × factor (wt%)

Atomic masses are on the Atomic masses sheet: match each one to its oxide by hand. Each step is then easy to check, and a slip shows up in one column.

Why are Cr, Ni, Sr and Zr not converted? They are in ppm because they are trace elements, a tiny fraction of the total mass: 104 ppm of Zr is only 0.0104 wt%. The oxygen bonded to them hardly changes the total, so they are not converted to oxides, and are reported as elements.

Worked example: from Cr₂O₃ to Cr

A rock contains 0.50 wt% Cr₂O₃. The formula has n = 2 chromium atoms and m = 3 oxygen atoms.

  1. Moxide = 2 × 51.996 g/mol + 3 × 15.999 g/mol = 151.989 g/mol.
  2. Factor: the share of the oxide's mass that is Cr = 2 × 51.996 g/mol ÷ 151.989 g/mol = 0.6842.
  3. Cr = 0.50 wt% × 0.6842 = 0.342 wt%.

Part 2

τ: correcting for mass loss

Uses the lecture: Mass loss concentrates immobile components, How τ helps, and Approach 2

A rise in concentration doesn't mean that anything was added. τ removes the effect of mass loss, by comparing each element with an immobile one: here, Zr.

τj = Cj,soil / CZr,soilCj,parent / CZr,parent − 1

C: concentration, in the units reported for that component (wt% or ppm). j: the element of interest. Both ratios use the same units top and bottom, so τ has no units.

Spreadsheet skill: what fill-down does to a reference

fx

Worked example: τ for sodium, from the lecture

Santa Cruz, 137 kyr terrace, at 2.52 m: Na₂O = 1.21 wt%, quartz = 53.5 wt%. Parent: Na₂O = 2.26 wt%, quartz = 42.0 wt%.

  1. Sample ratio: 1.21 wt% ÷ 53.5 wt% = 0.0226.
  2. Parent ratio: 2.26 wt% ÷ 42.0 wt% = 0.0538.
  3. τ = 0.0226 ÷ 0.0538 − 1 = −0.58: 58 % of the sodium has been lost, although the raw % change gives only 46 %.

The chemical index of alteration

τ needs a parent to compare with. The CIA doesn't: it describes how far one sample has weathered, from four oxides alone. It uses moles, because it is based on how much Al there is for each Ca, Na and K.

Worked example: CIA of the Ben Lomond granite, from the lecture

Al₂O₃ 16.96, CaO 5.74, Na₂O 3.34, K₂O 1.68 wt%, so 100 g of granite holds 16.96 g of Al₂O₃, and so on.

  1. Moles per 100 g: 16.96 g ÷ 101.96 g/mol = 0.1663 mol; 5.74 g ÷ 56.08 g/mol = 0.1024 mol; 3.34 g ÷ 61.98 g/mol = 0.0539 mol; 1.68 g ÷ 94.20 g/mol = 0.0178 mol.
  2. CIA = 100 × 0.1663 mol ÷ (0.1663 + 0.1024 + 0.0539 + 0.0178) mol = 49: about what a fresh granite should be.

How to set up a sheet that someone else could check
  • Leave the raw data as it is, and add your own columns to the right.
  • One step per column, with a clear header and units.
  • Keep constants, such as atomic masses, in their own table, and refer to them.
  • Add check cells: oxide totals should be close to 100, and τ for the reference must be 0.
A plot that someone else can read
  • Label both axes, with units. The default chart title rarely helps: say what the plot shows, or delete it.
  • Put depth on the y-axis, running downwards, as in the ground.
  • Use the same axis range on plots you want to compare.
  • Say so if an axis is logarithmic.
  • Keep the colours doing a job: one colour per series, and no 3-D.

Glossary

TermMeaning
Primary mineralsCrystallised with the original rock.
Secondary mineralsFormed at the surface by weathering: clays, iron and aluminium oxides.
Parent material, protolithThe original rock or sediment that the regolith formed from.
SaproliteChemically weathered rock that still has the rock's original structure.
RegolithEverything above fresh rock: soil and saprolite.
SoilThe top of the regolith: the O, A, E and B horizons, shaped by plants and animals.
Weathering frontThe depth over which a mineral goes from fresh to fully weathered.
ChronosequenceSoils of different ages on the same parent material, in a similar climate.
Mobile, slightly mobile, immobileHow readily an element or mineral is lost in solution during weathering: mostly lost, partly lost, or mostly retained. It depends on the conditions.
Residual enrichmentWhen mobile components are lost, the immobile ones make up a bigger share of the remaining mass.
Mass transfer coefficient, τThe fraction of an element gained or lost, after correcting for mass loss.
CIAChemical index of alteration: Al₂O₃ as a molar percentage of Al₂O₃ + CaO* + Na₂O + K₂O. A measure of how much Ca, Na and K have been lost, relative to Al.
